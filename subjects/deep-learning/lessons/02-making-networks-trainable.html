Deep learning / Module 02

Making networks trainable

Core lesson · Week 2 · Revised 3 November 2026

Outcomes and starting point

Module 1 showed how a network calculates a prediction, how backpropagation calculates gradients, and how a parameter update uses those gradients. Here we ask what happens when those operations are mathematically valid but training makes little progress. You should already be able to follow an affine layer, ReLU, the chain rule and a mean batch loss. Familiarity with training and validation splits is assumed.

By the end, you should be able to explain how initialisation and activation functions affect signal scale; trace why gradients can shrink or grow with depth; compare SGD, momentum and Adam; and explain the different roles of normalisation, residual connections, weight decay and dropout. Most importantly, you should be able to turn an observed training failure into a diagnostic hypothesis and a controlled comparison, while distinguishing trainability from generalisation.

We will use a deeper version of the inspection model from Module 1: numerical image features enter several hidden layers, and a final affine layer predicts a defect-severity score. All numerical examples and diagnostic observations below are illustrative. They are not measurements from a trained inspection model.

Why a larger network can fit the training data worse

Suppose a two-hidden-layer inspection model reduces training loss steadily, but a six-hidden-layer model barely improves. More layers give you more adjustable transformations, yet the training algorithm may struggle to find useful parameters. The distinction matters: a flexible model can represent useful functions without your chosen initialisation and update rule being able to reach them efficiently.

The entire training process has four cooperating parts. The architecture specifies the forward computation. The objective scores its predictions. Backpropagation differentiates that objective through the computation. The optimiser uses the gradients and its stored state to update parameters. Initialisation chooses where this process begins. Training therefore depends on more than whether the forward pass returns an output or the loss has a derivative.

As the model designer, you choose the number and width of the layers, the initialisation rule and the optimiser settings. These are hyperparameters, as is the dropout probability introduced later. Training updates the model’s weights and biases within the architecture you chose. The optimiser may also update running averages of gradients; those averages are calculated from the gradients, rather than selected by validation or fitted as prediction weights. Keeping these quantities separate will help us interpret the update equations.

For each batch, the order is forward computation, loss, backpropagation, then update. Repeat that cycle with new batches. During inference, the network calculates predictions without gradient-based updates; some components, notably batch normalisation and dropout, also change behaviour between training and inference.

A useful first distinction is between two problems you already recognise from conventional machine learning:

Observation What it suggests What it does not establish
Training loss remains high or becomes unstable Investigate implementation, objective, signal scale, gradients and updates It does not establish that the architecture lacks capacity
Training loss is low but held-out performance is poor Investigate generalisation, data leakage, distribution mismatch and objective–metric alignment A more powerful optimiser alone need not help
Training and validation both improve slowly Several explanations remain: small updates, difficult optimisation, insufficient capacity or a noisy objective The curves alone cannot identify the cause

Compare losses measured in comparable conditions. A training objective that includes a weight penalty differs from a validation data loss without that penalty. Dropout and batch normalisation can also make training-mode and evaluation-mode losses differ. Before interpreting a gap, know which computation each curve records.

To understand why an early layer may learn slowly, we will first follow the numbers travelling forward and the gradients travelling backward. Only then can we choose an intervention for a particular failure.

Depth multiplies the factors that carry a gradient

Follow one network before examining its derivatives

For one observation, let xx be the column vector of input features. The network has LL hidden layers, followed by an affine output layer that predicts one score. Let h0=xh_0=x. Hidden layer ℓ\ell receives the previous activation vector hℓ−1h_{\ell-1} and computes

zℓ=Wℓhℓ−1+bℓ,hℓ=ϕ(zℓ). z_\ell=W_\ell h_{\ell-1}+b_\ell, \qquad h_\ell=\phi(z_\ell).

Here, WℓW_\ell and bℓb_\ell are that layer’s weights and biases; zℓz_\ell contains its preactivations; and ϕ\phi applies an activation function entry by entry. If layer ℓ−1\ell-1 has nℓ−1n_{\ell-1} units and layer ℓ\ell has nℓn_\ell, then WℓW_\ell has shape nℓ×nℓ−1n_\ell\times n_{\ell-1} and hℓh_\ell has nℓn_\ell entries. Input values and activations depend on the observation; the same parameters are reused for every observation. The final prediction is ŷ=WouthL+bout\hat y=W_{\mathrm{out}}h_L+b_{\mathrm{out}}, and its loss is the scalar ℒ\mathcal L.

To update an early weight, we need to connect its change to the final loss. First consider one illustrative connection: a hidden activation aa enters a later unit whose preactivation is z=2a+bz=2a+b. Holding the connecting weight 2, the bias and the other inputs fixed, a change Δa\Delta a produces Δz=2Δa\Delta z=2\Delta a. Suppose the loss derivative at this later preactivation is 3. Then

Δℒ≈3Δz=3(2Δa)=6Δa. \Delta\mathcal L\approx3\Delta z=3(2\Delta a)=6\Delta a.

Thus the loss derivative with respect to aa is 3×2=63\times2=6. The weight supplies the rate from activation to later preactivation; multiplying by the later loss derivative connects that activation to the loss. These numbers illustrate the calculation, not fitted network values. If the activation is a=ReLU⁡(s)a=\operatorname{ReLU}(s), where ss is its preactivation, we must also multiply by the activation derivative: at positive ss it is 1, giving a derivative of 6 with respect to ss; at negative ss it is 0, giving zero. If the activation feeds several later units, add the contributions through those connections.

The matrix equation below collects these same per-connection calculations for a whole layer. Define δℓ=∂ℒ/∂zℓ\delta_\ell=\partial\mathcal L/\partial z_\ell: entry ii is the loss’s derivative with respect to preactivation ii in layer ℓ\ell. For adjacent hidden layers, the chain rule gives

δℓ=(Wℓ+1Tδℓ+1)⊙ϕ′(zℓ). \delta_\ell= \left(W_{\ell+1}^{T}\delta_{\ell+1}\right) \odot\phi'(z_\ell).

The symbol ⊙\odot means entrywise multiplication. Entry ii of Wℓ+1Tδℓ+1W_{\ell+1}^{T}\delta_{\ell+1} sums the later units’ loss derivatives multiplied by their connecting weights from activation ii. Multiplying that sum by ϕ′(zℓ)i\phi'(z_\ell)_i accounts for how activation ii changes with its preactivation. This is the same multiplication by 2 and then by the ReLU derivative in the scalar example, with contributions summed where there are several connections. Repeating this calculation explains how early-layer gradients accumulate products of weights and activation derivatives.1

For one observation, the corresponding weight gradient is

∂ℒ∂Wℓ=δℓhℓ−1T. \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial W_\ell} =\delta_\ell h_{\ell-1}^{T}.

This outer product has the same shape as WℓW_\ell. It reveals two reasons a weight gradient can be small: the backward derivative δℓ\delta_\ell can be small, or the incoming activation hℓ−1h_{\ell-1} can be small. For a mean batch loss, average these per-observation contributions. A small gradient is not automatically a fault; it is suspicious when a model with substantial remaining error receives too little useful update to reduce that error.

A scalar calculation makes the multiplication visible

To isolate the effect of repeated multiplication, choose a single illustrative path with 12 successive local derivatives, each equal to 0.50.5. These numbers are chosen for calculation, not measured from a network. A derivative of 1 at the end becomes 0.512≈0.0002440.5^{12}\approx0.000244 at the beginning. If each factor is 1.51.5, the result is 1.512≈129.751.5^{12}\approx129.75. These are examples of vanishing gradients and exploding gradients: derivatives repeatedly shrink or grow as they propagate through the computation.

A real network has many paths, matrix products and input-dependent derivatives. Some directions can shrink while others grow. The scalar calculation illustrates multiplication; it does not predict every gradient from the network’s depth alone.

The activation function affects those factors. A sigmoid activation σ(z)=1/(1+e−z)\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z}) has derivative σ(z)(1−σ(z))\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)), at most 1/41/4 and much smaller for large positive or negative preactivations. In those saturated regions, substantial changes in zz barely change the activation. The backward derivative therefore shrinks at that operation. ReLU has derivative 1 for positive preactivations and 0 for negative ones. It avoids positive-side saturation, but a unit that stays negative for all relevant inputs receives zero gradient through ReLU. Changing a negative unit’s incoming weights through that path cannot revive it while those derivatives remain zero.1,2

Large positive ReLU outputs are also unbounded. Replacing sigmoid with ReLU does not prevent large weight products from amplifying activations or gradients. Activation choice and parameter scale have to work together.

Initialisation sets the scale at which learning begins

Different units need different starting weights

If all hidden units in a layer start with identical incoming weights and biases, they calculate identical activations. In an otherwise symmetric network, they can receive identical updates and remain copies of one another. Random weights break that symmetry. Biases can often start at zero because distinct weights already make the units different.

The magnitude of those random weights matters as much as their differences. Consider one unit with nn inputs. Write its weighted sum as z=∑j=1nwjhjz=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_jh_j, choosing a zero bias for this calculation. We choose an initialisation rule that draws independent weights with mean zero and variance s2s^2, independently of the incoming activations. The variance describes the distribution of starting weights, not a constraint on their values after training. Let the incoming activations have a common second moment q=E[hj2]q=E[h_j^2], meaning their average squared magnitude. Averaging over the random initialisation and input distribution gives the approximate scale relation

E[z2]≈ns2q. E[z^2]\approx n s^2 q.

The second moment includes both variability and a nonzero mean. For instance, a constant activation of 2 has variance zero but second moment 4. We use the second moment because ReLU outputs generally have a positive mean. Under an approximately symmetric preactivation distribution, ReLU keeps half the squared magnitude on average, so

E[ReLU⁡(z)2]≈12ns2q. E[\operatorname{ReLU}(z)^2]\approx\frac12 n s^2q.

The factor nn appears because the unit sums nn weighted inputs. If the incoming width increases while weight variance stays fixed, that sum tends to grow. Choosing s2=2/ns^2=2/n approximately preserves the forward second moment for ReLU. This is He initialisation with a fan-in convention: fan-in means the number of inputs to a unit.2

Choose 64 incoming units for an illustrative calculation. This is a hypothetical width, not a recommendation or a value determined by the number of input features. The corresponding He weight standard deviation is 2/64≈0.1768\sqrt{2/64}\approx0.1768. To contrast it with a poorly scaled start, choose a smaller standard deviation of 0.050.05. That gives a second-moment multiplier of 64(0.05)2/2=0.0864(0.05)^2/2=0.08 per layer under this approximation. Across six such layers, the multiplier is 0.086=2.62144×10−70.08^6=2.62144\times10^{-7}. Tiny initial weights can therefore make activations rapidly collapse even though the network contains many parameters.

This calculation describes an initialisation model, not a guarantee for trained networks. Learned correlations, changing weight distributions, unequal widths, biases and other components alter the result. Preserving a forward average also does not guarantee that every backward direction has a useful scale.

Match the rule to the activation and architecture

Glorot, or Xavier, initialisation balances forward and backward scale with weight variance approximately 2/(nin+nout)2/(n_{\mathrm{in}}+n_{\mathrm{out}}) under its assumptions. Here, ninn_{\mathrm{in}} is fan-in and noutn_{\mathrm{out}} is fan-out, the number of receiving units. It is a common starting point for roughly linear or suitably scaled tanh layers. He initialisation compensates specifically for the loss of second moment through ReLU. The two prescriptions differ because they model different transformations.1,2

Use the initialisation prescribed for the actual architecture and activation rather than applying a hidden-layer rule to every tensor. Output layers can have different requirements. Standardising input features using training-set statistics also helps avoid an initial weighted sum being dominated by one feature’s units; it does not replace hidden-layer initialisation or justify using held-out data to fit preprocessing.

When a deep model begins with shrinking activation magnitudes and early-layer gradients, inspect initialisation and input scale before adding regularisation. An optimiser cannot reliably compensate for every layer starting in a poorly scaled state.

The optimiser turns derivatives into parameter changes

Backpropagation answers “how sensitive is this batch loss to each parameter?” It does not decide the step size or how much to trust a noisy batch. Let θt\theta_t collect the parameters before update tt, and let gtg_t be the gradient of the mean loss on that batch. The learning rate ηt\eta_t controls the update scale.

You choose the initial learning rate and any rule for changing it. To establish a working rate, compare a few multiplicatively spaced candidates for the chosen optimiser while keeping the data, architecture and update budget fixed. Inspect whether training loss falls stably, then use validation performance to compare useful candidates. There is no single suitable rate for all architectures, loss scales or optimisers.

SGD and momentum use gradients in different ways

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) uses

θt+1=θt−ηtgt. \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta_tg_t.

“Stochastic” refers to using sampled observations or batches rather than the full training dataset for each update. Even a correct batch gradient differs from the full-dataset gradient, so consecutive updates can point in different directions. A very small learning rate makes progress slow; a large one can repeatedly overshoot regions with lower loss or produce numerical divergence.

To see why a large derivative may require a small learning rate, consider the one-parameter loss ℒ(θ)=aθ2/2\mathcal L(\theta)=a\theta^2/2, where a>0a>0 controls curvature: larger aa means a sharper increase away from zero. Its gradient is aθa\theta, so SGD gives θt+1=(1−ηa)θt\theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta a)\theta_t. The magnitude shrinks only when 0<ηa<20<\eta a<2. A real network has multiple directions with different curvature; a step that works along a gently changing direction can overshoot along a sharply changing one. This quadratic calculation explains a local constraint, not a universal learning-rate bound for networks.

Momentum combines the current gradient with an accumulated gradient from earlier updates. Call the accumulated gradient vtv_t. You choose the retention factor μ\mu between 0 and 1: it multiplies the previous accumulation, so a larger value carries more of that history into the next update. Starting with v0=0v_0=0, we use the following update:

vt=μvt−1+gt,θt+1=θt−ηtvt. v_t=\mu v_{t-1}+g_t, \qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta_tv_t.

Repeated gradient signs reinforce the accumulated gradient, while rapidly alternating signs tend to cancel. Momentum can therefore make more consistent progress through noisy updates. It can also carry the model past a good region if the learning rate or retained accumulation is too large. Some descriptions instead use an average vt=μvt−1+(1−μ)gtv_t=\mu v_{t-1}+(1-\mu)g_t. For the same sequence of gradients, starting from zero with fixed μ\mu, that average is 1−μ1-\mu times the accumulation used here, so the same numerical learning rate produces a smaller step. Separate training runs can develop different gradients because their earlier updates differ. Check the equation, not just the name “momentum,” when comparing settings.10

Adam estimates a scale for each parameter

Two parameters can receive gradients of very different magnitudes. Adam keeps both an exponentially weighted mean gradient and an exponentially weighted mean squared gradient. Call them mtm_t and vtv_t; they start at zero. Here mtm_t is an array of running gradient averages, not the hidden width mm from Module 1. Adam’s vtv_t averages squared gradients, whereas the momentum variable above accumulates gradients without squaring them. You choose retention factors β1\beta_1 and β2\beta_2; larger values retain more of the previous estimates:

mt=β1mt−1+(1−β1)gt,vt=β2vt−1+(1−β2)gt2. m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t, \qquad v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2.

All squares and divisions in Adam are entrywise. Because both estimates start at zero, Adam corrects their initial bias using m̂t=mt/(1−β1t)\hat m_t=m_t/(1-\beta_1^t) and v̂t=vt/(1−β2t)\hat v_t=v_t/(1-\beta_2^t). The update is

θt+1=θt−ηtm̂tv̂t+ϵ. \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t- \eta_t\frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon}.

The small positive constant ϵ\epsilon prevents division by zero. For each parameter, a large running average of squared gradients produces a larger denominator, reducing its step relative to the mean gradient in the numerator. Adam adapts the step using that parameter’s gradient history; it does not determine which input features matter or guarantee generalisation.3

For a numerical comparison, choose a starting parameter θ1=1\theta_1=1, a first batch gradient of 2 and learning rate 0.10.1. The round numbers make the update sizes easy to compare; the learning rate is not a recommendation for an inspection model. SGD changes the parameter to 0.80.8. Momentum also changes it to 0.80.8 on its first step because the previous accumulation is zero. For Adam, choose β1=0.9\beta_1=0.9 and β2=0.999\beta_2=0.999, common starting settings rather than learned values. Then m1=0.2m_1=0.2 and v1=0.004v_1=0.004; bias correction gives m̂1=2\hat m_1=2, v̂1=4\hat v_1=4. Ignoring a negligible ϵ\epsilon, the new parameter is 1−0.1(2/2)=0.91-0.1(2/2)=0.9. The same numerical learning rate therefore does not imply the same parameter change across optimisers. Start with the intended implementation’s documented retention settings and compare changes when training behaviour warrants them.3,13

Method Useful starting reason Main caution
SGD A transparent baseline with few moving parts Can be slow or sensitive to a single global learning rate
SGD with momentum Gradient directions contain useful consistency across batches Accumulation changes the effective update scale and can overshoot
Adam Coordinate scales differ or gradients are noisy or sparse Still requires a learning rate; adaptation does not fix disconnected gradients or guarantee the best held-out result

Treat optimiser choice as a comparison to test under a stated budget. Counting epochs alone can hide differences in batch size and number of updates. Compare data exposure, update count, compute and the metric you actually care about.

Learning-rate schedules change the update scale over time

A single learning rate need not serve the whole run. A larger early rate may produce useful progress, while a smaller later rate can reduce oscillation and refine the fit. A schedule specifies ηt\eta_t over updates: step decay lowers it at chosen milestones, while cosine decay lowers it smoothly toward a chosen minimum. A warmup increases it from a small starting value to a target rate during the opening updates.

You choose the schedule’s duration, milestones and minimum rate; these are not values learned by backpropagation. Compare them within a fixed update budget after establishing a stable initial rate. Warmup can help when early activations or optimiser estimates are still settling, but it cannot reconnect a missing gradient. Decay that happens too soon can leave a poorly fitted model making very small updates. Record whether schedule steps count batches or epochs, since the same stated duration can otherwise describe different runs. For the original cosine schedule with warm restarts, see 11; a restart raises the rate again in a later cycle, whereas warmup describes its initial increase.

Normalisation controls intermediate values

Initialisation acts once. Hidden activations then change as parameters learn. A normalisation layer repeatedly centres and rescales selected groups of activations, giving later computations a more controlled input scale. The choice of group determines both the computation and the dependencies it introduces.

For a group of MM scalar values u1,…,uMu_1,\ldots,u_M, define its mean u‾\bar u and variance estimate su2s_u^2 by

u‾=1M∑i=1Mui,su2=1M∑i=1M(ui−u‾)2. \bar u=\frac1M\sum_{i=1}^M u_i, \qquad s_u^2=\frac1M\sum_{i=1}^M(u_i-\bar u)^2.

First centre and rescale each value to obtain ũi\tilde u_i. Then apply the learned scale and offset to produce the normalisation layer’s output yiy_i; here yiy_i denotes a layer output, not a training target:

ũi=ui−u‾su2+ϵ,yi=γũi+β. \tilde u_i=\frac{u_i-\bar u}{\sqrt{s_u^2+\epsilon}}, \qquad y_i=\gamma\tilde u_i+\beta.

The positive constant ϵ\epsilon makes the division defined even for a constant group. The network learns the scale γ\gamma and offset β\beta by backpropagation, just as it learns weights and biases. In contrast, the mean u‾\bar u and variance su2s_u^2 are calculated from the values in the group. The next two sections specify which values form a group and which outputs use each learned scale and offset. Because changing one group member also changes the calculated mean and variance, normalisation changes how derivatives connect the group’s values.

Batch normalisation uses other observations in the batch

Take a dense-layer preactivation matrix ZZ of shape B×mB\times m, where BB is the batch size and mm is the hidden width: rows are observations, columns are hidden units. Batch normalisation (BatchNorm) computes a separate mean and variance down each column during training. Thus each unit uses BB values, one from each observation. Its learned scale and offset are reused for that unit across all observations. In a convolutional layer, the usual grouping combines batch and spatial positions separately for each channel.4

For one hidden unit, choose a batch of three observations whose preactivations are 1, 3 and 5. The mean is 3 and the variance estimate is (4+0+4)/3=8/3(4+0+4)/3=8/3. Set γ=1\gamma=1 and β=0\beta=0 for this illustration so we can examine centring and scaling alone; these parameters can change during training. With negligible ϵ\epsilon, the first observation’s normalised value is (1−3)/8/3≈−1.225(1-3)/\sqrt{8/3}\approx-1.225. Change only the third observation’s preactivation from 5 to 9. The new mean is 13/313/3, the variance is 104/9104/9, and the first normalised value becomes −10/104≈−0.981-10/\sqrt{104}\approx-0.981. The value entering BatchNorm for the first observation did not change, but its training-time output did because the batch statistics changed.

In usual inference mode, BatchNorm uses stored running statistics accumulated during training, together with the learned scale and offset. It does not estimate the deployed observation’s statistics from the current inference batch. Consequently, incorrect mode selection, poorly estimated running statistics or a deployment distribution shift can damage predictions. Small or strongly correlated batches can also make training statistics unreliable; dense BatchNorm with just one observation has no useful within-column variation.

The original paper motivated BatchNorm partly through “internal covariate shift,” meaning changes in intermediate input distributions during training. Later experiments showed that reducing that proposed shift is not a sufficient explanation of its benefits; changes to the smoothness of the optimisation problem provide another account. Use the centring-and-scaling computation to reason about the layer, rather than treating its original motivation as a settled causal explanation.4,5

Layer normalisation uses features within one observation

For the same B×mB\times m matrix, layer normalisation (LayerNorm) computes statistics across the mm hidden activations in each row. One observation’s statistics therefore do not depend on other observations. Standard LayerNorm has a learned scale and offset for each normalised feature, reused across observations. It calculates each observation’s mean and variance from that observation in both training and inference.6

For a sequence tensor with shape B×T×dB\times T\times d, BB counts observations, TT counts sequence positions and dd counts features at each position. A common LayerNorm choice calculates a mean and variance across the dd features separately for each observation and position. It does not automatically normalise across time. Always identify the actual axes: “normalise the layer” alone does not specify the operation.

Neither operation replaces training-set input preprocessing, and neither guarantees better performance. LayerNorm’s independence from batch size is useful when batches are small, but it changes the representation differently from BatchNorm. The correct choice depends on which variation the task needs to preserve and which grouping the architecture expects.

Residual connections change the path through the model

A plain stack requires each block to produce the complete next representation. A residual block instead computes a correction and adds it to its input. Let hh be the incoming activation vector, and let F(h;θF)F(h;\theta_F) be a learned branch with parameters θF\theta_F. The block produces

hnext=h+F(h;θF). h_{\mathrm{next}}=h+F(h;\theta_F).

The two terms must have the same shape. When they do, a zero residual branch gives the identity map hnext=hh_{\mathrm{next}}=h. This gives the parameterised block a direct way to preserve an existing representation while learning a change. It helps explain why simply adding depth need not require every new block to relearn how to pass useful information onward.7

To see the derivative through a residual addition, first consider a single scalar input with an illustrative branch F(h)=0.2hF(h)=0.2h. The output is hnext=h+0.2h=1.2hh_{\mathrm{next}}=h+0.2h=1.2h, so a change Δh\Delta h produces 1.2Δh1.2\Delta h at the output. If the loss derivative there is 3, the input loss derivative is 3(1+0.2)=3+0.6=3.63(1+0.2)=3+0.6=3.6. The direct addition contributes 3 and the learned branch contributes 0.6. These contributions add because the same input affects both terms.

For vectors, the following equation collects those contributions. Define JFJ_F as the matrix whose entry (i,j)(i,j) is ∂Fi/∂hj\partial F_i/\partial h_j: it describes how each branch output changes with each input entry. Let II be the identity matrix. If gnextg_{\mathrm{next}} is the column-vector loss gradient at the block output, the input gradient is

gh=(I+JF)Tgnext=gnext+JFTgnext. g_h=(I+J_F)^Tg_{\mathrm{next}} =g_{\mathrm{next}}+J_F^Tg_{\mathrm{next}}.

One contribution passes directly through the addition without multiplying by the residual branch’s weights. However, the two contributions can reinforce or cancel. In a scalar example, F(h)=−hF(h)=-h gives derivative 1+(−1)=01+(-1)=0. A residual connection provides a useful route; it does not guarantee that the total gradient cannot vanish or explode.

If the input vector and residual output have different dimensions, choose a learned linear projection with matrix PP that maps the input to the required output dimension. For ninn_{\mathrm{in}} incoming entries and noutn_{\mathrm{out}} output entries, PP has shape nout×ninn_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{in}}. The shortcut then computes PhP h instead of passing hh through unchanged, and the block outputs Ph+F(h)P h+F(h). Its backward contribution uses PTP^T instead of an identity matrix. An activation after the addition also contributes its own derivative. These details matter when reading an architecture diagram: the simple identity-route equation applies to the addition exactly as written. Module 4 will examine how residual CNN blocks handle changes in spatial resolution and channel count.

Normalisation and residual connections can complement one another, but they solve different problems. Normalisation changes the scale and dependencies of a representation; a residual addition changes how representations and derivatives can pass through the architecture. Adding either without a diagnostic reason makes its effect harder to interpret.

Regularisation changes what training is encouraged to learn

Once the network can reduce its training loss, you still need useful predictions on new observations. Weight decay and dropout are tools for that problem, although both also affect optimisation. They are not default repairs for a network whose gradients are disconnected or whose activations have collapsed.

L2 penalties and weight decay coincide only in particular updates

Let ww collect the weights selected for regularisation, and let λ≥0\lambda\geq0 control its strength. Adding an L2 penalty gives the objective

𝒥=ℒdata+λ2‖w‖22. \mathcal J=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}+\frac\lambda2\lVert w\rVert_2^2.

The penalty’s gradient is λw\lambda w. Under plain SGD, with data gradient gtg_t, the update becomes

wt+1=wt−ηt(gt+λwt)=(1−ηtλ)wt−ηtgt. w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta_t(g_t+\lambda w_t) =(1-\eta_t\lambda)w_t-\eta_tg_t.

The penalty makes an improvement in data fit worthwhile only if it outweighs the extra cost of larger weights. It can discourage large coefficients that help fit incidental training variation, but strong penalties can also suppress relationships that would generalise. Under this plain SGD rule, the penalty is equivalent to shrinking weights by a common factor on each step. For an illustrative scalar weight, choose wt=2w_t=2, gt=0.5g_t=0.5, ηt=0.1\eta_t=0.1 and λ=0.01\lambda=0.01. Substituting these values gives the next weight:

wt+1=(1−0.1×0.01)(2)−0.1(0.5)=0.999(2)−0.05=1.948. w_{t+1}=(1-0.1\times0.01)(2)-0.1(0.5)=0.999(2)-0.05=1.948.

With Adam, adding λw\lambda w to the gradient also feeds that term into the moving averages and coordinate-wise scaling. It no longer produces the same uniform shrinkage. AdamW decouples weight decay from Adam’s gradient adaptation: Adam’s moments use the data gradient, and a separate term −ηtλwt-\eta_t\lambda w_t shrinks the selected weights. This distinction is why “Adam with an L2 penalty” and “AdamW” are different update rules.8

State which parameters receive decay: biases and normalisation scales are often excluded, but that is a design choice. Strong decay can prevent a useful fit. For scale-invariant normalised layers, shrinking weights can also change effective optimisation behaviour, so smaller weights alone are not a complete explanation of generalisation.

You choose λ\lambda rather than learning it as a prediction weight. The value 0.010.01 above makes the arithmetic visible; it is not a generally suitable penalty. Compare several strengths using validation performance, with the optimiser and loss reduction fixed, because their definitions affect how strongly the penalty acts.

Dropout changes the training computation

Dropout randomly removes selected activations during training, so later units cannot always rely on the same collection of incoming features. You choose the probability pp of dropping an activation; backpropagation does not learn pp in the setup described here. Let rir_i independently equal 1 with probability 1−p1-p, otherwise 0. In inverted dropout, training uses

h̃i=rihi1−p,0≤p<1. \tilde h_i=\frac{r_i h_i}{1-p},\qquad 0\leq p<1.

Conditional on the original activation, E[h̃i∣hi]=hiE[\tilde h_i\mid h_i]=h_i. Choose hi=4h_i=4 and p=0.5p=0.5 to make the two possible outcomes easy to compare: the training output is either 0 or 8, each with probability one half. Its average is 4. The probability 0.50.5 is illustrative, not a recommendation for every layer. At inference, inverted dropout leaves the activation unchanged; it does not randomly discard features or scale them again.9

Matching this activation’s expectation does not mean a nonlinear network’s deterministic prediction equals the exact average of all dropout predictions. Dropout introduces noise and changes the training objective in effect; it can improve robustness to reliance on particular activations, but can also slow fitting or cause underfitting. Its placement near BatchNorm can create a mismatch between noisy training statistics and inference activations. Use the intended architecture’s arrangement and measure the effect rather than adding dropout everywhere.

For an application, compare a no-dropout baseline with a few probabilities while holding other settings fixed. Use validation results to judge whether the extra noise helps; if training fit deteriorates without a held-out benefit, the chosen probability or placement is not helping that comparison.

Worked diagnosis: make a hypothesis distinguishable from alternatives

Return to the six-hidden-layer inspection regressor. For this example it receives 64 precomputed numerical image features, rather than Module 1’s two features. We choose width 64 for every hidden layer so the same fan-in calculation applies at all six layers, including the first. Matching width to input count is a teaching convenience, not a design rule. The hidden activations are ReLU, the output is affine, and the loss is mean squared prediction error. Suppose the training loss barely falls, hidden activations become much smaller with depth, and early-layer gradients are tiny. The following is a worked investigation of a hypothetical case.

Establish that the observations describe a real optimisation problem

First inspect data and the computation. Confirm that inputs and targets align, predictions and targets have matching shapes, the loss is reduced as intended, gradients are cleared between updates, backpropagation reaches the trainable parameters, and the optimiser actually updates those parameters. Check for nonfinite values. These implementation failures can imitate architectural problems.

Next, try fitting a fixed small subset in a separate diagnostic run with stochastic regularisation disabled. A model that cannot substantially reduce error on a learnable small subset warrants implementation, capacity or optimisation investigation before generalisation tuning. Success would show that this setup can fit those observations; it would not establish generalisation or the correctness of every other part of the pipeline.

For the hypothetical case, assume these checks pass. Input features are standardised using training data. The hidden weights were drawn independently with standard deviation 0.050.05, and hidden biases are zero. That makes the initialisation-scale explanation concrete enough to test.

Predict an observable consequence before changing a component

The hypothesis is that the hidden initialisation makes forward activations collapse, contributing to weak learning signals. Under the approximation derived earlier, each width-64 ReLU layer multiplies the activation second moment by 0.080.08. With input second moment 1, the predicted progression is:

Location Predicted second moment with standard deviation 0.05 With fan-in He initialisation
Input 1 1

These are calculated expectations under simplifying assumptions, not measured values. A finite network need not match them exactly. The prediction is a strong depth-related decline with the original rule and a substantially flatter initial scale with He initialisation.

Change one factor and compare the quantities the hypothesis predicts

Compare the original hidden initialisation with fan-in He initialisation, leaving the output-layer rule unchanged. Hold the architecture, dataset split, preprocessing, mean-loss convention, optimiser, learning rate, batch order and update budget fixed. For paired seeds, use the same underlying random draws and rescale the hidden weights according to each rule. Repeat across a small fixed set of seeds so a fortunate draw is less likely to determine the conclusion. The changed factor is the hidden initialisation rule.

Before the first update, record each layer’s activation root-mean-square value, which is the square root of its measured mean squared activation. During training, record the data loss, fraction of zero ReLU activations, per-layer gradient norms and actual parameter changes. A gradient norm measures the magnitude of all entries in a layer’s gradient; one useful choice is the square root of their summed squares. To interpret update size relative to existing parameters, record

ρℓ=‖ΔWℓ‖F‖Wℓ‖F+ϵ. \rho_\ell= \frac{\lVert\Delta W_\ell\rVert_F} {\lVert W_\ell\rVert_F+\epsilon}.

Here ΔWℓ\Delta W_\ell is the actual change from one update, the matrix norm means the square root of the sum of squared entries, and ϵ\epsilon only protects a zero denominator. There is no universal healthy value of ρℓ\rho_\ell. Compare its trajectory across layers and the two conditions. With Adam, actual updates are especially informative because raw gradient magnitudes do not directly determine step magnitudes.

If the new initialisation flattens the initial activation scales, increases useful early-layer updates and consistently improves training loss at the fixed budget, the results support this hypothesis. They do not prove initialisation was the only cause or establish better held-out performance. If activation scales improve but loss does not, investigate update size, output/loss compatibility, inactive ReLUs or other bottlenecks. If the scales were already stable, the initialisation hypothesis loses support; changing more components at once would hide that result.

Choose the next intervention from the remaining evidence

Remaining observation Plausible next comparison Reason and limitation
Loss spikes or diverges; updates are very large Reduce the learning rate with other settings fixed Tests overshooting; also check numerical and data errors
Finite but very large gradient norms precede spikes Compare with global gradient-norm clipping Limits occasional large updates; does not repair vanishing derivatives or diagnose their source
Scales are reasonable, updates are persistently tiny, loss falls slowly Test a larger learning rate within a bounded range Tests conservative steps; a larger rate may become unstable
Deeper plain model fits worse after scale and update checks Compare a suitable residual version Tests an architectural route to easier fitting; changes architecture, so use a separate comparison
Training fit is good but held-out error worsens Test weight decay or dropout individually; inspect data and validation Targets generalisation, with a possible cost to training fit

For gradient clipping, let gg collect the batch’s gradients and choose a threshold c>0c>0. Global norm clipping replaces gg by gmin⁡(1,c/‖g‖2)g\min(1,c/\lVert g\rVert_2) when its norm is nonzero. If the norm is at most cc, the multiplier is 1 and the gradient is unchanged. Above cc, it rescales the gradient to norm cc while preserving its direction. Choose a threshold by inspecting the finite gradient norms of the model and checking whether clipping curbs spikes without making most useful updates very small. There is no universal threshold independent of loss reduction and model scale; clipping cannot make nonfinite gradients trustworthy or recover gradients that are zero.12

The central skill is to write down an explanation that could be contradicted by the comparison. A falling loss after changing initialisation, optimiser, normalisation and dropout together would show that the new configuration works better under that run. It would not tell you which explanation was right.

What to carry into representation learning

Trainability depends on the entire chain from input scale to activation derivatives to actual updates. Initialisation establishes a starting scale; optimisers and schedules determine how parameters change; normalisation and residual paths modify the computation through which values and derivatives travel. Weight decay and dropout change the learning pressure and training behaviour, with potential benefits and costs for held-out performance.

Diagnose in that causal order when the evidence supports it: verify the computation, inspect scales and updates, make one controlled change, then assess both training fit and held-out behaviour. The ability to fit the training data and the usefulness of the learned representation are separate questions. Module 3 examines the latter: what information hidden features retain, what their similarities mean, and how the objective shapes that organisation.
